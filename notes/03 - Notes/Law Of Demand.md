# Law Of Demand / First Law of Purchase
## Core Definition

- States an **inverse relationship** between the **price** of a commodity($P_x$) and its **quantity demanded**.
- Assumption: **Other factors** affecting demand remain **constant** (Ceteris Paribus).
- If price increases( ⬆ ), quantity demanded decreases(⬇).
- If price decreases(⬇), quantity demanded increases(⬆).

---
## Ceteris Paribus Assumptions

### Constants in Demand Analysis

- **Price of Related Goods($P_r$)**: Prices of substitute and complementary goods do not change.
- **Consumer Income**($Y$): The income level of the consumer remains stable.
- **Tastes and Preferences($T$)**: Consumer preferences do not shift.
- **Future Expectations**($F$): No expectation of price changes in the future.

## Key Characteristics

### Inverse Nature
- Directly opposite movement between price and quantity.

### Qualitative Statement
- Indicates the **direction** of change in demand (increase/decrease).
- Does not indicate the exact **magnitude** of change.

### No Proportional Relationship
- Percentage change in price does not equal the percentage change in quantity demanded.

### One-Sided Relationship
- Analyzes how changes in price affect quantity demanded, not vice versa.

# Reasons for Law of Demand

The Law of Demand states an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

---
## Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility(DMU)

- As a consumer consumes more units of a commodity, the satisfaction (utility) derived from each additional unit decreases.
- Consumers are only willing to purchase additional units if the price decreases, matching the lower utility.

###### Example

|Unit|Marginal Utility (Satisfaction)|Price|Consumer Action|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|1st|High|$50|Buys|
|2nd|Medium|$50|Hesitates|
|2nd|Medium|$30|Buys|

---
## Substitution Effect

- When the price of a commodity falls, it becomes relatively cheaper compared to its substitutes.
- Consumers substitute cheaper goods for expensive ones.
###### Example
- Price of coffee increases, while the price of tea remains constant.
- Tea becomes relatively cheaper, leading to higher demand for tea and lower demand for coffee.

---
## Income Effect

- A decrease in the price of a commodity increases the consumer's **real income** (purchasing power).
	- Money Income : The nominal amount of money a person earns.
		- This is the **actual amount of money** a person receives in monetary terms (wages, salary, rent, interest, etc.). 
		- It’s measured in **currency units** and doesn’t account for changes in prices or purchasing power.
	- Real Income : The amount of goods and services a person can buy with their money income, after accounting for changes in prices.
		- This refers to the **purchasing power** of money income — i.e., how much goods and services you can actually buy with your money.- It adjusts for **changes in price levels (inflation or deflation)**.
		- Example: If your salary stays the same but prices of essentials rise, your _money income_ is unchanged, but your _real income_ has fallen because you can buy less.
- Increased purchasing power allows consumers to buy more of the same good or other goods.

---
## Additional Customers

- Lower prices make a commodity accessible to new customers who previously could not afford it.
- Higher prices may exclude some customers from the market.

---
## Different Uses

- Goods with multiple uses see increased demand when their price falls, as they are utilized for less essential purposes.
- When prices rise, users restrict consumption to essential purposes only.
###### Example
- **Milk:** If the price is low, milk is used for drinking, curd, butter, and sweets. If the price is high, it is used only for essential drinking.

# Exceptions to the Law of Demand

## Theory of Consumer Behaviour

### General Overview

- The **Law of Demand** generally states an **inverse relationship** between price and quantity demanded.
- **Exceptions** refer to situations where a **direct relationship** exists: price increase leads to demand increase, and price decrease leads to demand decrease.

---
### Giffen Goods
- A special type of **[[Demand#Inferred / inferior Goods|inferior good]]**.
- Demand rises when price increases(:arrow_up:) due to a strong negative **income effect** that outweighs the **substitution effect**.

- Originally identified by Sir Robert Giffen in the early 18th century, regarding bread consumption among low-income earners.
	- Price of bread ⬆ — demand for bread by low paid British wage earners ⬆
	- With rise in prices of goods, they were forced to cut consumption of meat and other expensive food items  
###### Example

|Item|Initial Price|New Price|Quantity Demanded|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|Bread|Low|High|Increases|
|Luxury|High|Higher|Decreases|

- **Reasoning:** As bread prices rise, poor consumers cannot afford alternative foods, forcing them to spend more of their limited income on bread to survive.

---
### Status Symbol Goods (Veblen Goods)

- Goods purchased to demonstrate wealth or social status rather than for their functional utility.
- Higher prices make them more desirable (**conspicuous consumption**).

###### Example
- **Diamonds**
- **Luxury cars**
- **Designer artwork**

---
### Fear of Shortage

- Consumers increase demand during price hikes if they expect prices to rise further or supply to disappear completely in the future.
- Driven by panic buying or hoarding behavior.

###### Example
- Hoarding essential commodities during lockdowns or natural disasters.

---
### Consumer Ignorance

- Consumers may perceive a higher-priced item as better quality, leading to higher demand for the expensive item due to lack of market knowledge.

###### Example
- A tourist purchasing a local product at a premium price without knowing the actual market price.

---
### Fashion Related Goods

- Goods currently in trend or fashion are demanded regardless of high prices.
- Delayed purchase might mean the good goes out of style.

###### Example
- Seasonal festival attire or limited-edition clothing.

---
### Necessities of Life

- Essential goods must be purchased regardless of price fluctuations.
- **Price elasticity of demand** is very low (inelastic).

###### Example
- **Medicines**
- **Salt**
- **Wheat**

---
### Change in Weather

- Specific commodities are demanded during certain seasons, regardless of price changes.

###### Example
- **Umbrellas** and **raincoats** during the rainy season.

---
# Summary

- **Law of Demand** defines an **inverse relationship** between price and quantity demanded.
- **Ceteris Paribus** (all other things equal) is a mandatory condition.
- Demand curves are **downward sloping**.
- The law is **qualitative**, not quantitative.
- **Law of Demand:** Inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.
- **Diminishing Marginal Utility:** Lower satisfaction from additional consumption requires lower prices.
- **Substitution Effect:** Consumers shift to cheaper alternatives.
- **Income Effect:** Lower prices increase purchasing power.
- **New Customers:** Lower prices increase the total customer base.
- **Multiple Uses:** Lower prices expand the utility scope of a product.
- **Exceptions** involve a direct relationship between price and quantity demanded.
- **Giffen goods** are essential inferior goods where price increases boost demand due to income constraints.
- **Veblen goods** increase in demand as prices rise to signal status.
- **Panic buying**, **ignorance**, **fashion trends**, **necessities**, and **seasonal changes** also bypass the law of demand.
